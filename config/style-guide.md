# Style guide — Organic's Best

> Derived from the full 215-article corpus on 2026-07-16 by automated analysis. PENDING HUMAN REVIEW — correct anything wrong; writers treat this file as authoritative.

## Voice

- **Audience:** New and expecting parents (predominantly mothers, but address as "parents"), anxious, time-poor, health-conscious, and researching formula/feeding decisions online. Many are first-timers looking for reassurance as much as facts. Skew toward buyers of European organic formula (HiPP, Holle, Kendamil, Kabrita, Jovie, Lebenswert, NannyCare).
- **Perspective:** Second person "you/your" throughout (dominant address by a wide margin), with the brand speaking as first-person plural "we/our" ("Our goal is to help you…", "we're here to support you"). Never lecture in the third person.
- **Register:** Warm-expert. Empathetic, reassuring, and conversational on the surface ("Let's face it…", "you're not alone", "you're doing a great job!"), but backed by specifics, brand comparisons, and cited figures. Encouraging, never clinical or alarmist — even health topics open with comfort before information.
- **Reading level:** Sentences run long and layered; measured reading level is roughly US grade 10–12 (Flesch ~49). The *tone* is casual but the *syntax* is not simple — aim to keep the warmth while trimming sentence length where you can.

## Structure conventions

- **Typical article length:** ~2,000 words is the norm (median 2,074; typical range 1,735–2,442). Flagship "Best … 2026" buying guides and deep health explainers run 4,000–6,000+. This has been stable across all years — do not pad short topics to hit a length.
- **Heading style:** Title Case, statement-form H2s (91% Title Case; only ~27% end in a question mark). Use questions selectively for genuinely FAQ-style sub-sections. H3s are used liberally for sub-points (roughly 1.3 H3s per H2). Comparison guides use "X vs. Y" and "Best … for …" heading patterns.
- **Opening pattern:** Empathetic hook, NOT answer-first. The reliable three-paragraph formula:
  1. A relatable scenario or worry that mirrors the reader's situation ("We know how worrying your baby's first illness can be…", "As a parent, you're probably no stranger to dirty diapers.").
  2. Reassurance / normalization ("you're not alone", "the good news is…", "that's perfectly okay").
  3. A roadmap sentence stating what the article will cover ("This guide explains…", "In this article, we explore…").
- **FAQs, tables, callouts, formatting:**
  - Bold is used in every article for emphasis and scannability — bold key terms, brand names, and takeaways.
  - Ordered/unordered lists appear in nearly every article; use them for symptoms, steps, and rankings.
  - Tables are near-universal (a "Table of Contents" block sits near the top of long guides; comparison tables carry brand/spec comparisons).
  - An explicit FAQ section appears in roughly a third of articles — include one when readers have discrete follow-up questions.

## Language rules

- **Words/phrases we use:** "little one" / "little ones" (the signature term for the baby — used ~930 times across the corpus), "tummy/tummies", "journey", "peace of mind", "rest assured", "the good news is", "you're not alone", "we're here to…", "keep reading". Warm openers like "Let's…". Use these naturally; do not stuff.
- **Words/phrases we avoid:** Clinical detachment, fear-mongering, and absolutes. Avoid overusing em dashes (the corpus barely uses "—"; it favors commas and en dashes "–" in ranges/headings). Do not shame any feeding choice. Do not make unqualified medical or health claims (see hedging below).
- **How we refer to caregivers:** Default to **"parents"** (by far the most common; ~950 uses) and direct-address **"you"**. "Moms/mothers" appears when a passage is specifically about maternal experience (e.g., postpartum, breastfeeding, milk supply) — acceptable there, but do not make "mom" the default reader. "Caregivers" is rare; include it only for inclusivity when it fits. Occasional dedicated content addresses dads directly.
- **Medical hedging style:** Heavy and consistent. Normalize the reader's situation first, then defer to a professional. "Consult your pediatrician" is the house phrase (used ~500 times); "healthcare provider", "seek medical care", and "this is not medical advice" also appear. Place a hedge whenever giving guidance that touches health, dosing, symptoms, or formula changes.
- **Regulated-content handling (WHO Code — mandatory):**
  - Every article acknowledges breastfeeding superiority. When relevant, cite that "the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months," then normalize formula/combination feeding without judgment ("over half of parents turn to formula… and that's perfectly okay").
  - Never denigrate or discourage breastfeeding; never claim any formula is equal or superior to breast milk. Present formula as a valid, well-supported choice, not a replacement upgrade.
  - Health/nutrition claims are qualified ("may help", "is designed to", "often") and paired with a professional-consultation hedge — avoid guarantees of medical outcomes.
  - **Standard closing footer (append verbatim to every article — present on ~96% of the corpus). Exact format, fixed 2026-09-23: two paragraphs, this wording and punctuation, no heading, last block before References; never paraphrased or merged:**

    > Please be aware that this information is based on general trends in babies, and it is not medical advice. Your doctor should be your first source of information and advice when considering any changes to your child’s formula and when choosing your child’s formula. Always consult your pediatrician before making any decisions about your child’s diet or if you notice any changes in your child.
    >
    > Breastfeeding is the best nutrition for your baby because breast milk provides your child with all the essential nutrients they need for growth and development. Please consult your pediatrician if your child requires supplemental feeding.

- **Meta title/description patterns:** SEO titles ~56 characters, often with a hook or a curiosity angle ("The Epic Face-Off: HiPP vs Holle"). The brand suffix "… | Organic's Best" appears in the corpus but is **not added by default** to new articles — only when the manager asks for it. Hard limits: title **≤600px**, description **≤960px** as Google renders them (~70 / ~155 characters as proxies). Meta descriptions ~150 characters and almost always end with a CTA imperative and an exclamation mark ("Read more now!", "Learn now!", "Click to read more!") — ~87% carry a CTA, ~81% end in "!". Match this: benefit + CTA + "!".
